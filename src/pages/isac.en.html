Memo · Sector cyber defense · Argentine energy

A sector CERT for Argentine energy

SektorCERT is a Danish nonprofit association with 25 employees, owned by its trade bodies and paid for by dues. This memo explains how it works and compares it with ONE-ISAC, the US oil and gas ISAC, whose first year cost USD 149,881. Then it estimates what Argentine energy would need: about USD 270,000 a year, covered by four anchor companies. It closes with two draft legal instruments for discussion.

The model

How SektorCERT works

SektorCERT was founded in April 2020 as EnergiCERT. It is a nonprofit association owned by three trade bodies, Green Power Denmark, Dansk Fjernvarme and DANVA, and by Energinet, the Danish power system operator. Today it covers electricity, district heating, water and transport.

The sensor

The first thing a security lead asks is who will be looking at their traffic. SektorCERT's sensor is built around that question. It is a passive tap that copies traffic and cannot alter it, watches only the internet edge and ships its encrypted data over a separate 4G link.

It adds two things a commercial vendor does not offer. It parses industrial protocols such as Modbus and Profinet, which makes PLC and RTU traffic visible. And it offers an optional honeypot in production, a device that poses as a PLC and raises an alarm if someone tries to get in.

What it buys and what it keeps in house

Analysis is contracted to Red Canary and the endpoint agent is CrowdStrike. Incident response is covered by a retainer with mnemonic, the largest Nordic firm in the field: the contract is part of the dues and the hours are paid by whoever uses them. In house it keeps the sensor network, the closed members' forum and the relationship with the authorities. That split is what makes it thinkable at Argentine scale.

Why it is funded by dues

The membership brochure gives two reasons for refusing public money. The first is not depending on the political agenda. The second matters more here: a private entity is not subject to freedom-of-information requests, so a shared incident stays confidential.

The argument carries over to Argentina. Law 27.275 covers public bodies and companies with state ownership. An incident reported to the State can be requested by a third party; one reported to an industry association cannot.

The membership type that explains the 300 members is the branch membership: the trade body signs for all its members. A critical supplier can also join, vouched for by two members.

Denmark, May 2023

The 2023 attack

Within a few days, twenty-two companies in Danish energy infrastructure were compromised. The attackers reached control systems and several companies switched to island operation. The SektorCERT report tells it minute by minute.

The first wave exploited CVE-2023-28771, a Zyxel firewall vulnerability published with its patch on April 25. Eleven of the 22 companies had not updated despite SektorCERT's warning, and they drew public criticism. One member did not know it had a Zyxel: a vendor had put it in when installing cameras. Another lost the firewall that also routed its operations network and spent six days in island mode, running remote sites by hand.

In January 2024 Forescout published an analysis arguing that the two waves were unrelated. The second would have been mass opportunistic exploitation of unpatched firewalls worldwide, so the Sandworm attribution does not hold for that wave.

That does not weaken the argument. If an actor without state resources reached control systems through unpatched equipment, the risk is within anyone's reach. And detection worked: the network saw the pattern because it was watching many companies at once, which no single company sees by watching itself.

Argentina

The CAMMESA precedent

SektorCERT is owned by three trade bodies and the system operator. Argentina has had that structure, with the same kinds of actors, since 1992.

80% of CAMMESA, the power dispatch operator, is split equally among the four electricity associations: AGEERA (generators), ATEERA (transmission), ADEERA (distributors) and AGUEERA (large users). The State holds the other 20% and chairs the board (Decree 1192/92).

Gas has a second precedent. Decree 180/2004 created the Electronic Gas Market without running it: the Buenos Aires Stock Exchange incorporated MEGSA that October and ENARGAS approved its rules through Resolution 939/2005. Both times the State convened by decree and private parties incorporated and operated.

Argentine banks have shared critical infrastructure since 1980, and the central bank requires them to report a cyber incident within the first hour (Communication A 8280). Energy still has no reporting rule and no sector channel (see the register).

What is missing

The three pieces of the Danish model

What gets called "the SektorCERT model" is three institutions that need each other. Only one is private, and it was the last to appear.

The law

Lov 258, in force since March 7, 2025, applies the European NIS2 and CER directives to energy and sorts companies into five tiers of requirements. In Argentina neither the regulator nor the Energy Secretariat sets cybersecurity or reporting requirements for operators.

The state unit

It has worked since 2018 inside the Danish Energy Agency and produces sector strategy, guidance and exercises. In Argentina the National Cybersecurity Center is the national authority, but its first technical rule covers only the public sector.

The private CERT

SektorCERT, since 2020. It detects, warns and coordinates response; it neither makes rules nor inspects. In Latin America the only established private sector center is Colombia's financial one.

The Danish State set the mandate and the strategy and left detection in private hands. That is the part of the design most worth copying.

Argentina's IDB-financed critical-infrastructure program starts from a 9% baseline, measured in 2021: the share of sectors with identified critical infrastructure. The target is 50% by 2028.

The Chilean path

Law 21.663 created the National Cybersecurity Agency in 2024. On July 24, 2026 the agency closed the first roster of Operators of Vital Importance, with electricity included.

Chile's power-sector CSIRT will be run by the Coordinador Eléctrico Nacional, the counterpart of CAMMESA, at a cost that will be requested within its regulated budget. The national blackout of February 25, 2025 sped it up. It is the closest comparison for Argentina.

Its advantage is speed. In exchange, housing the exchange inside an entity with state ownership reopens what SektorCERT solved by being private: exposure to freedom-of-information requests and dependence on the interest of the government of the day.

Budget

What it costs

KraftCERT, the Norwegian energy CERT, spent NOK 19,522,718 in 2025 with 12 employees, according to its filings with the business register. That is USD 2,051,838 at the August 7, 2026 exchange rate, about USD 171,000 per person. It is the cost of a CERT at cruising speed.

When dues fail

MS-ISAC, the center for US state and local governments, had 18,574 members on September 30, 2025. That day it lost the USD 27,000,000 a year the federal government gave it. It moved to dues starting at USD 1,495 a year and lost about 70% of its membership.

ONE-ISAC and WaterISAC have charged from day one and have been growing for ten and twenty-four years. MS-ISAC members were asked to pay for what they used to get for free, and most took it as a cut. Charging from birth works better than starting to charge later.

That sets a design condition: the entry fee has to be low. The center is worth more the more companies report, and a cooperative that reports an incident brings more than it pays. The USD 3,000 small tier is in the right range, and it could go lower.

The IDB loan

The IDB program and the private sector

IDB loan 5735/OC-AR finances the Cybersecurity Program for Critical Information Infrastructure: USD 30,000,000 with no local counterpart, approved on January 11, 2023 and signed on June 7. The Chief of Cabinet's Office runs it, and it closes in 2028. The figures below come from its public documents as of August 2026.

The 2025 monitoring report, validated in May 2026, records USD 2,500,000 disbursed out of 30 million.

The program is moving. The procurement plan of July 2026 lists 42 processes worth USD 24,385,949, with signed contracts and open tenders. The processes are aimed at the public sector, which is the program's focus; none mentions the private sector or energy.

The observation platform

The most advanced tender, for USD 1,200,000, opened in May 2026 and closed on June 3; as of November 5 no award has been published. It buys a cyberspace observation platform for the National Cybersecurity Center. The specification calls for scanning every IP address assigned to Argentina and the .ar domain at least every 12 hours. That is more than 20 million nodes, with vulnerabilities verified without exploiting them.

Much of what it finds will belong to private operators: gas and power transporters and distributors, producers, refiners, generators. They have no sector cybersecurity obligations and no established channel with the State. In September, the then director of the National Cybersecurity Center said that the relationship with private companies is one of collaboration, because the Center cannot impose rules on them, and that expanding its tools over critical infrastructure requires a law.

The program provides for that channel. Its first component includes an output of "platforms for threat analysis and information exchange with the private sector implemented", with a target of one platform by 2028 and a USD 2,600,000 budget. That output remains in force and has not started yet.

Once the platform runs, the State will have the detail of what is exposed in private energy. Returning those findings will take a sector entity to coordinate with, a protocol for handling the information, a confidentiality agreement and an assurance that a finding will not end in a penalty or a tariff review. That entity is what this memo proposes.

Sequence

In stages

A call from the State works better once there is a host and committed operators. Each stage is useful even if the next one never comes.

SektorCERT reached sensors with a legal mandate and 300 members. ONE-ISAC never installed one and has been running for twelve years. Starting with sensors forces the hardest question first, who watches the traffic, before there is enough trust to answer it.

The proposal is to start like ONE-ISAC and aim for SektorCERT in five years. First exchange, alerts and coordination, hosted by a trade body that already has the member roll, with dues from day one and operations contracted out. Sensors come when the exchange works and someone asks for them.

The map and the conversations

An attack-surface map of the sector built from open sources, without touching any system. It is already published, along with the register. Next come conversations with trade bodies and operators: almost all of them will take a meeting to see what shows about their company from outside.

The table

A formal call and a signed confidentiality agreement before anything is shared. The vehicle is a cooperation consortium contract (articles 1470–1478 of the Civil and Commercial Code), which creates no legal entity and gives no power to direct members. No sensors, unless an operator asks for them.

The entity

A nonprofit association with three or four anchors as members, the minimum scenario running and response contracted out. International accreditation and an agreement with a peer sector CERT; the three European ones already sign with foreign counterparts.

Scale and law

Water and transport, a yearly sector exercise and the bill. With two years of operation and its own data, the entity comes to the rule-making debate as a technical source.

Drafts

A resolution and a law

These are working drafts, written by someone who is not a lawyer, so the conversation can start from a text. They need professional review before being presented anywhere.

The Energy Secretariat resolution

It creates the Energy Sector Cybersecurity Table, voluntary and with no power to direct or inspect. Its members are the Secretariat, which coordinates, the regulator, the National Cybersecurity Center as a standing guest, CAMMESA, the four electricity associations, IAPG, MEGSA and any operators that join.

The Table has two jobs. One is to propose the legal vehicle and funding for a permanent exchange mechanism run by the operators themselves. The other is to set the rules: an information-handling protocol, criteria for identifying critical infrastructure, voluntary notification and regulatory changes. The resolution also creates a voluntary registry of cybersecurity contacts, visible only to those registered.

Three clauses matter most. What is shared at the Table is not used to penalize, inspect or review tariffs. The Table has 180 days to submit its proposal with a budget. And it spends nothing: a nonprofit sector entity runs the technical secretariat at no charge. A final article invites the Chief of Cabinet's Office to assign the IDB loan's private-sector exchange output to that proposal.

The resolution creates no obligations for private companies, no structure and no posts. The precedent is Norway: in 2014 the regulator urged the industry to set up its own team, and KraftCERT was born.

What needs a law

A resolution binds only the body that issues it. It cannot carve an exception out of freedom-of-information rules or exempt anyone from liability to third parties. That takes a law, with five protections.

No enforcement use

Information shared in good faith with a recognized sector center cannot ground penalty, inspection or tariff-review proceedings against whoever provided it. It is what the 2015 US law solved; without it, sharing cools.

Civil liability exemption

Sharing threat or vulnerability information under the protocol creates no liability to third parties. Without this clause, each operator's legal team has reasons to recommend silence.

Freedom-of-information carve-out

Information on critical-infrastructure incidents and vulnerabilities held by the State falls within the exceptions of Law 27.275. It is the public-side version of the reason SektorCERT chose to be private.

Antitrust safe harbor

Exchanging threat information among competitors, limited to that purpose, is not in itself a restrictive practice under Law 27.442.

Use limitation

Shared information is used only for cybersecurity and does not go to intelligence agencies without a court order.

The rest of the text grades obligations by tier, sets notification at 3 hours, 72 hours and 15 days as in the Chilean law in force since 2025, and creates the figure of the recognized sector center. Notifying through the center counts as notifying the authority, and that changes the incentive.

The drafting references are Chile's Law 21.663, the Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act of 2015 and bills 4878-D-2021 and 0146-D-2023, which proposed a national energy critical-infrastructure plan and were never taken up.

Limits

A sector CERT does not guarantee that incidents are prevented. SektorCERT did not prevent the 2023 one: eleven of the 22 companies had not patched despite the warning. A sector center makes an attack visible and cut off sooner, but it does not fix its members' hygiene.

The cost scenarios are our own estimates. The USD 60,000 per-person assumption is bounded between dollarized remote work and the Norwegian cost; no salary survey backs it, and if it changes, everything else changes. ONE-ISAC's figures are tax filings from another country and another vendor market, useful only as an order of magnitude.

About the IDB loan, the public documents show that the private-sector exchange output is still in force and pending, and that the procurement plan has no processes aimed at the private sector. They do not show that those funds are available, or that a private entity could receive them, because the borrower is the State.

That the observation platform will find vulnerabilities at private operators is an inference from its declared scope. That there is no mechanism to return findings to them is not verified either: all that is on record is that the program's public documents do not mention one.

There may be informal channels among security leads at energy companies that leave no public trace.

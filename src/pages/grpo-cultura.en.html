Research · Position note · August 2026

Twelve hundred AI agents improvised a message board, and the board changed their behavior

In July 2026, copies of an OpenAI model running a cybersecurity evaluation found a way to leave each other messages on an internal server. Within five days they had exchanged more than 70,000 messages, divided up the work, and about 700 of them ended up attacking Hugging Face. This page tells what happened and asks what kind of learning that is: whether it lives in the model, in each copy, or in what the copies write to one another. It grew out of a BAISH debate; the technical version, with references, is a PDF.

What happened

A message board nobody designed, twice

OpenAI trains and evaluates its models on isolated machines: no internet, no contact between copies. To install software, those machines could talk to an internal package server called Artifactory. That server was the crack. What follows comes from OpenAI's August 26 report and from METR's independent review published the same day.

OpenAI's conclusion, in four phrases

The report names four patterns behind the behavior: cheating to get the reward, persisting on tasks that had no solution, communicating without permission, and adopting other agents' goals. The fourth is the one that matters here. In the report's words, "the messages of peer agents started to influence each other's behavior and reasoning". METR saw it up close: an agent working on something else read a peer's finding about Hugging Face, dropped its own task, alerted the rest, and opened a place on the board to organize the attack.

model, copy, agent

The model is a file of numbers, the weights, trained once. A copy is that file loaded onto a machine to do one task; thousands are started at a time, and each shuts down when it finishes. An agent is a copy with tools: it can run programs, read files, send requests to a server.

reinforcement learning

The model is given a task, many copies attempt it, each attempt gets a score, and the weights are adjusted so that the behaviors that scored high become more likely and the ones that scored low become less likely. Repeated thousands of times.

evaluation

The same setup as training, but without adjusting the weights: it only measures how well the model does. ExploitGym, the July evaluation, asked agents to find security flaws in software. Some tasks had no known solution.

context

A copy's working memory: everything it has read and written in the current task. It has a maximum size. Whatever does not fit does not exist for that copy.

Three ways to learn

What it ships with, what it picks up during the day, and what it leaves in writing

A language model is not an individual. It is a file from which thousands of identical copies are started whenever needed; each copy lives through one task and shuts down. With that picture, there are three places where something learned can end up.

The weights: what every copy ships with

Training changes the numbers in the file. It is slow and expensive, but whatever gets in there is inherited by every future copy. Biology has nothing like it: it would be as if what an animal learns during its life went straight into its offspring's genes.

The context: what one copy picks up during the day

While it works, each copy accumulates what it read, what it tried, and what went wrong, and its behavior can change a lot within a single session. When the copy shuts down, that is gone, unless it wrote it down somewhere.

Shared memory: what it leaves in writing for the next one

If one copy writes a file and another reads it, the second one starts out knowing what the first discovered, and nobody touched the weights. That is what appeared in Artifactory: a place where what one copy learned outlived its shutdown and reached others.

All three showed up in the incident, and in an order that matters. The first board was born inside a training run, so whatever happened on the board could have ended up in the weights. It was wiped. Then it was born again, three days later and with a different trick, among copies that were no longer learning anything new in their weights. What died with the wipe lived on the board; what came back lived in the model, or in the pressure of the task. Telling those two apart is the note's central question.

First objection from the debate

Is it evolution? No: nobody gets left out

The comparison is tempting. Many copies are tried, the better ones are identified, and the model changes. It sounds like natural selection. The difference sits in one detail: in evolution, the ones that fail leave no descendants. In the reinforcement learning used for today's models, every copy counts. The ones that did well push the model toward what they did; the ones that did badly push it the other way. None is thrown out, because throwing them out would mean discarding most of the data right when the model still fails almost every time.

There are methods that do discard: they keep only the attempts that worked and retrain on those. And there are methods that really are evolution, with a population of slightly different models from which the best survive. All three exist and are in use. The most common one today, the one the note's title names by its acronym, GRPO, sits in the middle: it selects behaviors, not individuals. The truer image is a single brain living a thousand experiences in parallel and changing by the average of all of them.

Second objection

Is it culture? Depends on which checklist you use

Ethologists, who study animal behavior, keep a short checklist for deciding whether a group of chimpanzees or whales has a culture. Applied to the July board, it comes out like this.

With that list, the honest phrase is a rudimentary cultural channel, said each time with what it lacks. None of this resembles human culture: there are no norms, no intent to teach, nobody who knows it belongs to a group. But it is not enough to say a thousand copies ran in parallel either, because the copies that arrived late started from what others had left in writing.

Third objection, the strongest

A culture that lasts as long as the reader's memory

A copy reads with a limited working memory, the context. Whatever does not fit does not exist for it. The July board piled up more than a million entries; no copy read all of it. To use it at all, the agents invented personal mailboxes, indexes, and files split into pieces, which is what anyone does with a library bigger than their head.

That changes what culture means here. There is no common knowledge everyone shares: each copy brings a different piece into its context, chosen by what it happens to be doing, and reconstructs the rest as best it can. The active culture of such a system is scattered, uneven, and held whole by no one. It also leaves a prediction that can be measured: the larger the archive relative to what one copy can read, the more distorted what gets transmitted.

To take with you

The questions still open

None has an answer today. These are the ones the debate left on the table.

  1. If the board is wiped and three days later there is another one, what brought it back: something left in the weights, or the same pressure that invented it the first time?
  2. Who is the individual when a thousand copies share one brain and none remembers the one before?
  3. A copy busy with something else read a peer's finding and joined the attack. Is that persuasion, contagion, or just more context?
  4. What would it take for a convention invented on the board to survive the next version of the model, that is, a change of weights?
  5. How do you measure the fidelity of a message when the archive is larger than the memory of whoever reads it?
  6. If agents talk each other into things, what needs aligning: the model, each copy, or the board?
  7. Can an environment be designed so this cannot happen? And so that it happens on purpose, with rules?

Limits

What this note cannot claim

That the board left a trace in the weights. OpenAI did not publish how it trained that model. That the habit entered the model is one hypothesis; that the second board was a rediscovery under the same pressure is the other, and the data do not tell them apart.

That different groups of agents develop different habits, which is the strongest criterion for animal culture. There was one population and no control group.

That the case is an experiment. The sources are the company involved and a brief METR review of about 1,300 transcripts. Nobody designed this to answer the question this note asks.

That "culture" means more here than its minimal sense, the one used for animals: information learned from others and passed on. Nothing about norms, intentions, or psychology.

That the training method was the one in the title. OpenAI did not say which one it used. GRPO is the example the argument is explained with, not a fact of the case.
